Urban Heat Response Planning

City heat plans work best when public health teams, transit agencies, utilities, and neighborhood organizations coordinate their response before temperatures rise. The strongest plans identify who is responsible for each action and how residents will hear about it.

Recommendations for local leaders

Prioritize cooling access in neighborhoods with low tree cover, older housing, and limited access to air conditioning. Extend the hours of libraries and community centers during dangerous heat periods, and provide reliable transportation to those locations.

Coordinate transit service with cooling sites. Keep routes to public cooling centers operating during evening hours, publish service changes in multiple languages, and establish a clear process for requesting accessible transportation.

Share heat alerts through trusted local organizations as well as city channels. Messages should state the expected conditions, the actions residents can take, where to find help, and when the next update will arrive.

Measure readiness

Track the opening hours of cooling locations, transit availability, and response times for requests for assistance. Review these measures after each heat event with neighborhood representatives and update the plan before the next season.

Regional coordination matters when heat crosses city boundaries. Neighboring communities can share equipment, staff, and public information so that residents receive consistent guidance while moving between jurisdictions.
